New Leaf

Operations Dashboard

My parents’ landscaping company, rebuilt as software—so the knowledge in their heads and spreadsheets can become a system they can actually hand off.

How this started

This is my parents’ company—one they’ve been trying to get me to take over since I was little. Running the landscaping business was never the path I saw for myself. I like building software: systems, data, and interfaces that make messy work clearer.

But this was a way I could help. I started by consulting with them on what the business actually needed. As they’re getting older they want an exit strategy, and the hard truth is the business still depends on the knowledge, memory, and manual work they put in every day. They needed a way to automate, make things consistent, and build better, easier processes than the ones they were stuck with.

Build timeline

1

Phase 01 · Foundation

Reporting & a real database

Problem

They went into each season mostly blind—printed spreadsheets of pricing, houses, and worker hours, with no clean way to compare one year to the next. Critical numbers lived in folders and printouts, not in a system.

Solution

I connected their QuickBooks account, stood up a database shaped around how the business actually runs, and built a reporting tab that joins customers, invoices, and line-item prices to work-record hours by invoice number. Year-over-year labor and billed amounts show up in one place—per job, per service.

Jobs report · hours and billed · year over year

Tech

Grants and RLS policies land together; QuickBooks tokens stay service-role only and encrypted. Nothing hits production until migrations have run on a separate dev project.

2

Phase 02 · Record keeping

Work records that don’t fall through cracks

Problem

Their master spreadsheet—no real column names, treated like scripture—was the bible for everything. Jobs with open questions, unbilled work, and incomplete rows were easy to lose. Entering data was error-prone and disconnected from invoicing.

Solution

I imported that history into the database, then built a work-record view so they can filter by date and invoice status, flag needs-attention jobs, and see what still needs billing. Next came a structured input flow: batch by crew, enforce cleaner fields, and suggest QuickBooks line items so invoicing gets easier instead of restarting from scratch each week.

3

Phase 03 · Field ops · in progress

Dispatch instead of verbal directions

Problem

Crews depended on my parents for direction—what to do, where to go, and in what order. Schedules lived in conversation. There was no shared day list tying employees to crews, crews to trucks, and trucks to stops.

Solution

I built a dispatch board: assign employees to crews, crews to trucks, and trucks to ordered day lists. Pull up a service like spring cleanup, drag stops from an unassigned parking lot onto a crew, reorder mid-day, and send lists to the field.

Still building next:

  • Location-aware ordering by property lat/long for more efficient routes
  • Field work records pre-filled from dispatch—workers only enter arrival and departure times

Where this leaves them

Year-over-year visibility, work records that match how invoicing actually works, and dispatch that turns a day of stops into something you can send and reorder—in the same system.

The quieter win is optionality: an exit strategy that doesn’t require the next owner to inherit unlabeled spreadsheets and tribal knowledge.
