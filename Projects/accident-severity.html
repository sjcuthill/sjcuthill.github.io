Accident Severity Prediction

IST 418 Final Project · PySpark & Python

We started with a set of assumptions about what makes a crash severe. Then we built a model, and it disagreed with most of them.

01
The Question

What actually makes a crash severe?

Everyone has a gut answer. Bad weather, low visibility, busy intersections, stop signs and crossings. Our group had those same inferences going in, and the U.S. Accidents dataset gave us a way to test them instead of just repeating them.

The goal was a model that predicts accident severity (a score from 1 to 4) from weather, roadway, and location features, plus a clear picture of which of those features the model leaned on. If the model could rank them, we could check our own assumptions against it.

02
The Setup

A Spark session, sized for the job

The data was large enough that plain pandas wasn’t the right tool, so I built the pipeline on PySpark in Google Colab. I mounted Google Drive, read the sampled accidents CSV with schema inference, and gave the driver 4 GB of memory up front because cross-validation was coming later and I knew it would be hungry.

# driver memory raised for cross-validation later on spark = SparkSession.builder.appName('Final Project') \ .config("spark.driver.memory", "4g") \ .getOrCreate() accident_df = spark.read.csv(file_path, header=True, inferSchema=True)
03
The Cleanup

Making raw data model-ready

Raw accident records don’t go straight into a model. Most of the work in this project happened here, and I inspected the schema and a sample of rows after every step to make sure each transformation did what I thought it did.

  • Booleans to binary. Thirteen roadway flags (Amenity, Bump, Crossing, Give_Way, Junction, No_Exit, Railway, Roundabout, Station, Stop, Traffic_Calming, Traffic_Signal, Turning_Loop) cast to integers.
  • Day/night to 1/0. Sunrise_Sunset mapped from text to a binary feature.
  • Zip codes. Extended ZIP+4 values had their dashes stripped and were cast to numbers so they could be used as a location feature.
  • Rounding. Latitude, longitude, and crash distance trimmed to sensible precision.
  • Dropping dead weight. Removed ID, end coordinates, airport code, source, end time, and the twilight columns, either because they were fully null or added nothing.
  • Nulls. Rows missing any model input were dropped before assembling features.
04
Know the Target

Look at severity before predicting it

Before training anything, I grouped by Severity and computed each level’s share of the dataset. That mattered. A skewed target changes how you read every result afterward, and this one is heavily skewed toward severity 2. You can see it in the overlay histogram later on, where the real severity values pile up at 2 and thin out at 3, 4, and especially 1.

05
The First Model

A decision tree regressor

I used a Decision Tree Regressor on severity. A tree is easy to inspect, and it exposes feature importances directly, which was the whole point of the project.

The pipeline combined 22 inputs into a single features vector with a VectorAssembler: crash distance, zip code, six weather measurements (temperature, humidity, pressure, visibility, wind speed, precipitation), the thirteen roadway flags, and day/night. Then an 80/20 train/test split with a fixed seed so results were repeatable, a fit, and a first look at predictions and RMSE.

06
Tuning

Cross-validation, not guessing

Rather than accept default settings, I ran a grid search with 3-fold cross-validation over tree depth (2, 3, or 4) and minimum instances per node (1 or 2), scored on RMSE. That is 6 parameter combinations across 3 folds, so 18 fits, which is why the extra driver memory earned its keep.

paramGrid = ParamGridBuilder() \ .addGrid(dtr.maxDepth, [2, 3, 4]) \ .addGrid(dtr.minInstancesPerNode, [1, 2]) \ .build() cv = CrossValidator(estimator=dtr, estimatorParamMaps=paramGrid, evaluator=evaluator, numFolds=3)

I pulled the best model’s settings back out, saved the tuned model to disk, and reloaded it to confirm it round-tripped. Then I looked at its feature importances and printed the full tree structure to see the actual decisions it made.

07
What It Leaned On

Three features did almost all the work

Here is what the tuned tree actually relied on. Crash distance, zip code, and traffic signals carry about 96% of the model’s importance between them. Add stop signs and it’s about 99%. Every other feature, including temperature, humidity, visibility, and wind speed, scored exactly zero.

Feature importance, full model

  • Distance(mi)0.4507
  • Zipcode0.2820
  • Traffic_Signal0.2312
  • Stop0.0250
  • Precipitation(in)0.0070
  • Pressure(in)0.0039
  • 16 other features0.0000

One honest caveat: my grid capped tree depth at 4, so the tree can only ever split on a handful of features. Zero importance here means “the shallow tree never needed it,” not “this feature has no relationship with severity.” That’s one more reason the isolated models in chapter 10 were worth building.

08
Checking Our Hunches

The correlations told the same story

Before trusting the model, I went back to the raw data to test our original inferences directly. If weather and road features really drove severity, they should show up as correlations with it. They barely do.

I also plotted the raw relationships. Severity against temperature shows every severity level spread across nearly the whole temperature range, with no band that belongs to a worse crash. Temperature against visibility, colored by severity, is a thick overlapping cloud with no clean separation between levels. The scatter also surfaced a few obvious sensor errors, readings below −75°F and above 200°F, which a threshold-based tree shrugs off.

09
How It Predicted

Reading the model’s mistakes

Feature importance says what the model used. The error plots say how well it worked, and they were the most revealing graphs in the project.

That column of dots is the key finding. The model never predicts a 1, 3, or 4. It behaves like a regressor that has learned the dominant class and nudges its guess slightly based on distance, zip code, and signals. With a target this skewed, that is what a shallow tree trained on RMSE tends to do.

The honest takeaway: the model can tell you where the average crash sits. It can’t yet tell a rare severe crash from an ordinary one.

10
The Plot Twist

Pulling the features apart

Weather and road features ranked far lower than we expected, so we turned the surprise into a second experiment. If they’re near zero in the full model, what do they do on their own? I built two more models with exactly the same recipe (vector assembler, 80/20 split, decision tree regressor) so the outputs would be comparable.

Weather on its own

With nothing else to lean on, the weather model spreads importance across all seven features. Pressure leads at 45%, then wind chill at 21%. Precipitation, the feature most people would bet on, ranks dead last at 5%. Pressure is probably acting as a stand-in for where and when a crash happened, rather than as a cause of anything, but that’s my read, not something the model proves.

Roads on their own

The road model is far more concentrated. Five flags carry about 99% of the importance, led by Crossing at 55% and Traffic_Signal at 24%. Something interesting: in the full model, Crossing scored zero. On its own it’s the best road signal there is, which suggests that once distance and zip code are available, the tree finds crossings redundant.

11
Takeaways

What I took from it

96%

of full-model importance sits in distance, zip code, and traffic signals.

~1%

is what all weather features add together once distance and location are in.

2.0–2.5

is the only band the model ever predicts, on a 1 to 4 severity scale.

  • Test your assumptions. The most useful result was the one that contradicted our starting hypothesis. Weather barely moved severity, in the correlations or in the model.
  • Location beats conditions. How far a crash stretched and where it happened told the model far more than the temperature or the road signage around it.
  • Read the error plots, not just the metric. The residuals and the predicted-vs-actual chart showed that the model was hugging the majority class, something one RMSE number would have hidden.
  • Imbalance is the next problem. With severity this skewed, the real next steps would be class weighting or resampling, and a classifier that’s judged on recall for severe crashes.
  • Interpretable beats clever. A shallow, tuned decision tree let us explain the “why,” which a black box would not have.
  • Isolate to understand. The weather-only and road-only models turned a surprising result into a clear explanation.
